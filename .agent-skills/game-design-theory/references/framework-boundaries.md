# Game design theory framework boundaries

## Start from the decision

A framework is useful only when it changes one design decision, causal explanation, or
prototype. Begin with observed evidence and a controllable variable. Do not start by trying
to fill every framework box.

## MDA

Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek describe Mechanics, Dynamics, and Aesthetics as different design
levels. The designer works from rules and systems toward behavior and experience, while the
player encounters the experience before discovering the underlying rules.

Use MDA when:

- a mechanic is expected to produce a runtime pattern;
- the runtime pattern is expected to support a named experience;
- the team can test the links separately.

Limits:

- a mechanic can produce several dynamics;
- a dynamic can be interpreted differently across players and contexts;
- named aesthetics do not prove that the target players experienced them;
- the framework does not supply universal tuning values or a study design.

## Motivation needs

Self-determination theory work in games examines experiences such as autonomy, competence,
and relatedness. Use these as hypotheses about why a particular interaction may support or
undermine motivation in a specific context.

Limits:

- the needs are not a content checklist;
- more choice does not automatically produce meaningful autonomy;
- difficulty alone does not prove competence support;
- a social feature does not automatically create relatedness;
- behavioral engagement alone cannot identify the underlying experience.

## Decision structure

Map what the player knows, the available options, costs, timing, reversibility, uncertainty,
and consequences. A dominant strategy exists only relative to the tested states and player
goals. Do not call an option dominant because it was popular in one build or cohort.

Use this lens for unclear choices, one-option play, tactical depth, hidden information, and
risk/reward questions. Pair observed choices with the information and incentives players
actually had.

## Feedback and resource loops

Map sources, sinks, stock, conversion, gates, and delayed effects. Classify the hypothesized
loop as reinforcing or balancing, then test whether the full loop exists in play.

Limits:

- a diagram is not evidence that players perceive or use the loop;
- delayed effects can hide or invert the expected short-term signal;
- multiple loops may counteract each other;
- adding a sink or reward can change motivation and decision quality, not only quantities.

## Learning and mastery

Inspect what players can perceive, predict, practice, transfer, and combine. Separate a rule
that is hard to discover from an execution that is hard to perform. Use errors, retries,
explanations, demonstrations, and transfer tasks as evidence rather than designer intent.

## Bartle taxonomy

Richard Bartle's original types emerged from observations of players in multi-user dungeon
contexts. It can be a limited vocabulary for an evidenced multiplayer question. It is not a
universal personality test, immutable segment, or sufficient product strategy.

Do not use a label to infer needs, monetization tolerance, accessibility, or preferred content
without direct evidence.

## Ethical boundary

A design can increase a metric while undermining informed choice, wellbeing, accessibility,
or trust. Record dark-pattern risk, vulnerable-player impact, data collection, and the cost of
the intervention. Treat engagement metrics as behavior, not proof of positive experience.
